# Special Teams

Special teams is a third of the game's phases and gets about three percent of the analysis. That's a mistake this chapter aims to fix. Kicks and punts are where field position — the quietest input to win probability — actually changes hands, where the 2024 rulebook revolution happened, and where a single blocked kick can swing a game by two possessions' worth of expected points.

## The Field Goal / Extra Point Operation

A placekick is a three-man ballet performed in about **1.3 seconds**: long snapper to holder (roughly 8 yards behind the line) to kick, with eight blockers holding a wall just long enough. Anything slower gets blocked; anything sloppier — a laces-in spot, a low snap — turns a 90% kick into a coin flip.

Field goal distance math, worth internalizing: **distance = line of scrimmage + 17** (7–8 yards back to the spot, plus 10 yards of end zone). Ball at the opponent 30 = a 47-yard attempt. Extra points snap from the 15-yard line, making every PAT a 33-yard kick — that's been true since 2015, when the league moved it back to make the automatic point slightly less automatic (~94% league-wide, and meaningfully worse in December wind).

| Kick | Spot | Approx. success |
|---|---|---|
| Extra point | Snap from the 15 (33-yard kick) | ~94% |
| FG under 40 | — | ~90%+ |
| FG 40–49 | — | ~80% |
| FG 50+ | — | ~70%, rising every year |

Those last two rows are the story of the modern kicking era. Justin Tucker's 66-yarder in 2021 set the record; by 2024–25, Brandon Aubrey was drilling 60+ yarders for Dallas like mid-rangers and coaches were sending kickers out from distances that used to be automatic punts. **The practical effect: "field goal range" moved back nearly 10 yards in a generation, which rewrites fourth-down decision charts between the 35s.** A team with Aubrey or Tucker-in-his-prime effectively plays on a longer field than its opponent — that asymmetry is real, priced inconsistently, and worth checking before betting a total on the [Betting](/betting) board.

The miss costs more than three points, too: on a missed field goal the opponent takes over at the **spot of the kick**, so a missed 55-yarder hands over the ball near midfield. Long attempts are genuine gambles, not free rolls.

## Punts and Directional Punting

The punt is a voluntary trade: possession for roughly 40 yards of field position. The unit is the punter, a long snapper, a shield of blockers, and two **gunners** split wide whose job is to sprint 50 yards through double-teams and arrive with the ball.

What separates punting units is not gross yardage — it's **net** yardage and placement:

- **Hang time vs. distance:** a 55-yard line drive with 3.8-second hang is worse than a 45-yarder with 5.0 hang, because the first one gets returned 15 yards. Great punters trade distance for air.
- **Directional punting:** aiming for the sideline third of the field, forcing the returner to field it on the move near the boundary — or not at all. A punt out of bounds at the 8 cannot be returned by anyone.
- **The pin-deep game:** inside the 20 ("coffin corner" out of bounds, or pooch punts with gunner downing). The difference between an opponent starting at their 20 versus their 8 is significant — backed-up offenses call conservative plays, and a three-and-out from the 8 often gives the punting team the ball back in scoring position. That compounding loop is how punters win games without anyone noticing.
- **Touchback avoidance:** a punt into the end zone comes out to the 20, erasing everything past the 20-yard line. Punters who spray touchbacks are giving back hard-won yards.

The Ray Guy Award-caliber punters — modern examples like the Niners' Mitch Wishnowsky era of Aussie-style directional punting, or AJ Cole in Las Vegas — function as defensive weapons: they manufacture long fields, and long fields manufacture punts and turnovers at the other end.

## Kickoffs: The 2024+ Dynamic Kickoff

For a decade the league strangled the kickoff for safety reasons until returns nearly vanished. In 2024 it did something remarkable instead: it redesigned the play from scratch. The **dynamic kickoff** — borrowed from the XFL — was adopted for 2024, then made permanent (with a big tweak) in 2025.

![Dynamic kickoff alignment and coverage lanes](/knowledge/kick-coverage.svg)

The setup:

| Element | Rule (2024+) |
|---|---|
| Kicker | Kicks from his own 35, alone |
| Coverage unit | Other 10 players line up at the *receiving team's* 40 |
| Setup zone | At least 9 return-team players between their 30 and 35 |
| Returners | Up to two, deep in the landing zone / end zone |
| The freeze | Nobody but kicker and returners may move until the ball hits the ground or a player in the landing zone |
| Landing zone | Receiving team's goal line to the 20 |

Then the outcome table, which is really a set of incentives:

| Where the kick lands | Result |
|---|---|
| In the landing zone (goal line–20) | **Must be returned** |
| Lands in landing zone, rolls into end zone | Return it or take a touchback at the **20** |
| Caught/lands in the end zone, kneeled | Touchback at the **30** (2024) → **35** (2025) |
| Out the back of the end zone | Touchback at the 30 (2024) → 35 (2025) |
| Short of the landing zone | Treated like out of bounds — receiving team ball at its **40** |

The design logic is elegant: with coverage players starting only 25-ish yards from the returner and frozen until the catch, there's no 40-yard sprint and no violent car-crash collisions — but there's also no dead air. The 2025 touchback move from the 30 to the **35** was the league putting its thumb on the scale: kicking it out of the end zone now costs you five more yards than the old rules, so teams actually kick returnable balls. Return rates, which had cratered under the old rules, jumped dramatically — the kickoff became a real football play again, and return specialists like KaVontae Turpin became roster-priority players rather than luxuries.

**Onside kicks** are now a declared play: under the dynamic rules a team must announce the onside attempt (traditional alignment applies), originally restricted to trailing teams in the fourth quarter in 2024, loosened in 2025 so trailing teams can declare one at any point in the game. The surprise onside kick — one of football's great chaos plays — is effectively extinct, which is a real (if small) change to late-game comeback math.

## Returns

Return jobs are specialist crafts:

- **Kick returners** under the dynamic rules need vision and burst more than top-end speed — the play hits fast, lanes are defined by the coverage's fixed starting spots, and a returner who presses the wedge and makes one cut can hit a crease at full speed. Starting field position swings of 15–20 yards per return are routine, and a single house call flips a game.
- **Punt returners** have the harder judgment job: catch it clean with gunners bearing down, fair catch, or let it bounce? The decision tree (return / fair catch / peel away and let it roll... hoping it reaches the end zone) is worth points per season all by itself. A muffed punt is among the most catastrophic single plays in football — live ball, short field, disaster.

Devin Hester's 2024 Hall of Fame induction — the first true return specialist enshrined — was the league formally admitting what analysts always argued: elite returning is a genuine, game-swinging skill, not a novelty. The dynamic-kickoff era is likely to mint more careers like it.

## Hidden Yardage: Why This Phase Matters More Than Fans Think

Here's the analytics case for special teams in one idea: **every possession's expected points is set by where it starts.** A drive from your own 20 is worth a bit under a point in expectation; from midfield, roughly two; from the opponent's 20, well over four. Special teams is the market where those starting points are bought and sold.

"Hidden yardage" is the ledger of all of it: net punt differential, return yardage, touchback decisions, penalties on kicks, missed vs. made field goals versus expectation. A team that wins hidden yardage by 8–10 yards per possession swing is getting something like a field goal of expected value per game that never shows up in offense/defense stats — and coaches know it, which is why special teams coordinators are disproportionately represented among head-coaching hires (see the staff trees on [Coaches](/coaches)).

For bettors and fantasy players specifically:

- **Kicker quality moves totals and teasers.** A great kicker converts dead drives into 3s; a shaky one turns red-zone trips into zeroes. Same offense, different points.
- **Punter quality is a defense stat in disguise.** Long fields depress opponent scoring — it shows up in the defensive numbers, but the punter did it.
- **Weather amplifies everything.** Wind games (check the [Schedule](/schedule) for outdoor venues) hit kicking first and hardest — a 15 mph crosswind can take 10 yards off field goal range and turn punts into adventures. Unders in wind are a cliché because the mechanism is real.

## Blocked-Kick Mechanics

Blocks aren't luck — they're engineered, and they come in two flavors:

**Interior (A-gap) blocks** attack the middle of the protection, where the ball's flight is lowest. Field goal protection puts enormous stress on the snapper and the guards; a rusher who gets penetration into the **block point** — roughly a yard in front of the spot, where a normal kick's trajectory is still climbing — only needs to get a hand up. This is why long snappers are full-time professionals: a snapper who has to think about a nose tackle on his head is a snapper whose snap arrives late, and late snaps get kicks blocked.

**Edge blocks** run a rusher off the corner on an arc to the block point. The geometry is knife-edged: the edge rusher must beat the wing's block *and* flatten to the spot in about 1.3 seconds, which is why teams put their fastest, longest athletes on the field-goal-block unit and why a slow operation (holder bobble, kicker's extra half-step) is fatal.

Punt blocks work the same way with a deeper block point (the punter stands ~14–15 yards deep) and a different tradeoff: overloading one gap for the block means abandoning return lanes, so coordinators pick "block on" or "return on" calls, rarely both.

The payoff justifies the design work: a blocked kick is a multi-possession swing — points denied, field position flipped, and (on blocked FGs and punts) frequently a short-field or walk-in score the other way. Blocked kicks and return touchdowns are the two biggest single-play win-probability spikes special teams can produce, and they are exactly the plays a good unit *manufactures* a couple of times a season.

## The Takeaway

Special teams is where rules innovation is happening fastest (the dynamic kickoff is the biggest on-field redesign in decades), where field position — the input every efficiency stat silently depends on — actually gets set, and where roster spots 46 through 53 earn their keep. Watch one game charting nothing but starting field position after every kick, and you'll never call it the boring phase again.
