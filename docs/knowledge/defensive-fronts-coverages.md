# Defensive Fronts & Coverages

Defense is a coordinate system. Every defensive lineman aligns at a numbered "technique," every gap has a letter, and every coverage is a named answer to the same question: who takes the deep part of the field, and who takes the receivers? Learn the numbering and the shell names and defensive film stops being chaos — you'll see a 3-technique knifing into the B-gap, a safety rotating late into Cover 3, a nickel corner carrying a seam because the call was quarters-match. This chapter builds the system from the ground up: fronts first, then coverages, then the modern blurring of both.

## Technique numbers: where linemen align

Defensive line alignment is described relative to the offensive line, using a numbering system usually credited to Bear Bryant. Even numbers are head-up on a blocker; odd numbers (and "i" shades) are offset into a gap.

![Defensive technique alignments 0–9](/knowledge/front-techniques.svg)

| Technique | Alignment | Classic occupant |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| 0 | Head-up on the center | 3-4 nose tackle |
| 1 | Shaded to center's shoulder | Nose in even fronts |
| 2 / 2i | Head-up on guard / inside shoulder of guard | Run-stuffing DT |
| 3 | Outside shoulder of guard | The premier pass-rushing DT spot (Aaron Donald's home) |
| 4 / 4i | Head-up on tackle / inside shoulder of tackle | 3-4 defensive end |
| 5 | Outside shoulder of tackle | 4-3 defensive end (run downs) |
| 6 | Head-up on the tight end | Strong-side end |
| 7 | Inside shoulder of the TE | Old-school strongside technique |
| 9 | Outside the tight end, wide | Speed rusher (the "wide-9" — Micah Parsons, Myles Garrett on obvious pass downs) |

The gaps between blockers get letters: **A** (center–guard), **B** (guard–tackle), **C** (tackle–TE or edge), **D** (outside the TE). Sound run defense is simply this: every gap has exactly one assigned defender. When a linebacker "fits" a run, he's filling his letter. When a run breaks big, some gap went unmanned — usually because a lineman got washed out of his gap or a second-level defender guessed wrong.

Two structural terms:

- **One-gap vs. two-gap**: a one-gapping lineman attacks one gap and lives in it (penetration, chaos, the Donald model). A two-gapper controls the blocker head-up and plays *both* gaps beside him (the classic 3-4 nose, e.g. Vita Vea absorbing double teams). Most modern defenses are hybrids — two-gap the nose, one-gap everyone else.
- **Odd vs. even front**: an **odd front** has a man over the center (0/1-tech) — the 3-4 and its nickel descendants (like the popular 3-3-5 "Tite" front with 4i ends that walls off the B-gaps against inside zone). An **even front** leaves the center uncovered — the 4-3's over/under fronts. "Over" shifts the line strength toward the TE; "under" shifts it away.

## 4-3 vs. 3-4 — and why base defense barely exists

On the depth chart, defenses still call themselves 4-3 (four linemen, three linebackers) or 3-4 (three linemen, four linebackers). The classic distinction: 4-3 ends rush with a hand in the dirt; 3-4 outside linebackers stand up and can rush or drop, with the trade-off that 3-4 linemen historically two-gapped to keep their linebackers clean.

Here's the modern reality: **base personnel is a situational package, not an identity.** Offenses live in 11 personnel, so defenses live in **nickel** (five DBs, ~two-thirds of league snaps) and use **dime** (six DBs) on passing downs. In nickel, the 4-3/3-4 distinction nearly evaporates — everyone fields four or five "front" players and six or seven coverage players, and the same edge defender who'd be a "3-4 OLB" on Monday's depth chart is a wide-9 end on 3rd-and-8. What actually distinguishes modern fronts is the structure question (odd vs. even, who's covered), the gap philosophy (one-gap vs. two-gap), and how many true linebackers stay on the field. The rise of do-everything hybrid safeties (the Derwin James / Kyle Hamilton role — box player, slot corner, and deep safety in the same game) exists precisely to keep base-defense sturdiness inside a nickel shell.

## Coverage shells: the numbers game

Coverages are named for the number of *deep* zone defenders. Before the snap, count safeties; after the snap, watch where they go — that's most of coverage identification. The deep math is a fixed budget: every defender deep is one fewer defender near the line.

![Cover 1 through Cover 4 deep shells](/knowledge/coverage-shells.svg)

| Coverage | Deep | Underneath | Man/zone | Strength | Weakness |
|----------|------|-----------|----------|----------|----------|
| Cover 0 | 0 | all man | Man, no help | Maximum pressure — usually a 6+ man blitz | Any receiver who wins is gone; one missed tackle = TD |
| Cover 1 | 1 (FS in middle) | man + 1 hole/robber | Man with a ceiling | Tight coverage plus a run-support extra | Crossers, rubs, elite slot receivers |
| Cover 2 | 2 half-field safeties | 5 zone | Zone | Flats and picks jumped by squatting corners | Deep middle "hole," high-lows on the corner |
| Tampa 2 | 2 + MLB runner | 4 zone | Zone | The MLB carries the deep middle, patching C2's hole | Asks a linebacker to run 25 yards; intermediate middle voids |
| Cover 3 | 3 (corners + FS) | 4 zone | Zone | Sound vs. run (8 in the box), sound deep | Four verts (4 on 3), flood, seams |
| Cover 4 (quarters) | 4 | 3 zone/match | Match | Doubles verticals; safeties free to rob runs and digs | Flats open early; play-action can bait the safeties |
| Cover 6 | 3 (quarter-quarter-half) | 4 | Split-field | Tailors each half to the formation | The seam/flat conflict on the half side |

Notes worth keeping:

- **Cover 2's "hole"** between the safeties is why the smash concept and the seam from #2 exist. **Tampa 2** — the Dungy/Kiffin signature with Brian Urlacher-style linebackers sprinting down the middle — was the 2000s patch for it.
- **Cover 3** is the coverage of eight-man boxes: the strong safety drops down, the FS plays centerfield. It's the safest way to be strong against the run and not naked deep, which is why the Legion of Boom Seahawks built a dynasty-adjacent defense on it (much more in the Defensive Scheme Families chapter).
- **Cover 6** is the reminder that NFL coverage is *split-field*: coordinators routinely call one coverage to the field/strength and another to the boundary — quarters to the two-receiver side, Cover 2 to the single-receiver side.

## Man vs. zone vs. match — the third category that runs the league

The man/zone binary is how fans learn coverage; **match** coverage is how the NFL actually plays it. Match (pattern-matching) starts as zone and converts to man based on the routes that show up — "zone eyes, man principles." It was born (in football lore, from Nick Saban's time with Belichick in Cleveland) to solve four verticals, which shreds spot-drop zone.

The two match families to know:

- **Rip/Liz (match Cover 3)**: Saban's single-high answer. The seam defenders don't just wall the seams — they *carry* any #2 receiver who runs vertically, converting to man on him, while the coverage stays zone against short breaks. "Rip" and "Liz" are simply the right/left calls for which way the coverage spins. This is how a Cover 3 team survives four verts.
- **Quarters match (e.g. "Sky"/"Mod" reads, Saban's "Rat"/2-Read family)**: the safeties read the #2 receiver. If #2 goes vertical past ~10 yards, the safety takes him man-to-man; if #2 breaks out or shallow, the safety zones off and doubles #1 with the corner. Result: quarters can *double both outside receivers* against verticals while still throwing nine defenders at the run when #2 blocks. This coverage-that's-both is why two-high football took over — the full story is in the defensive schemes chapter.

How to tell them apart on film: in spot-drop zone, defenders backpedal to landmarks and stare at the QB. In man, defenders open their hips and stare at their receiver. In match, defenders start like zone and then suddenly "lock on" mid-play — the tell is a defender passing off a shallow route, then sprinting to carry a vertical.

## How offenses identify coverage pre-snap

Quarterbacks run a fast diagnostic, and you can run it from your couch:

1. **Count the deep safeties.** One high → Cover 1 or 3. Two high → Cover 2, 4, or 6. None → Cover 0 or a disguised rotation.
2. **Check the cornerbacks' leverage and eyes.** Corners squatting at 5 yards with eyes inside on the QB → zone (C2/C3). Corners pressed, eyes locked on the receiver → man. Corners at 7 with outside leverage → often quarters.
3. **Use motion as a truth serum.** Defender chases across the formation → man. Zones bump → zone. (Defenses lie sometimes; it's still the best single test.)
4. **MOF open vs. closed.** The pro shorthand: **M**iddle **O**f **F**ield **closed** (a safety in the deep middle) = 1/3 family; **MOF open** (two safeties splitting the hash area) = 2/4/6 family. Every passing concept has a MOF-open answer and a MOF-closed answer, and the QB's first post-snap job is confirming the pre-snap guess — because modern defenses **rotate late**, showing two-high and spinning to single-high (or vice versa) at the snap precisely to poison this diagnostic.

## Pressure: blitz vs. sim vs. creeper

The last layer is the rush. Vocabulary:

- **Four-man rush**: the default. The dream is affecting the QB with four so seven can cover — the healthiest way to play defense, if you have the rushers.
- **Blitz**: five or more rushers. Extra rushers come from linebackers or DBs ("cat"/corner blitzes, safety pressure). The cost is coverage — a blitz usually means Cover 1, Cover 0, or a thinned-out fire-zone.
- **Fire zone**: the classic zone blitz — five rush, three deep, three under, with a defensive lineman dropping into coverage where the QB expects a throwing lane. The Dick LeBeau Steelers institutionalized it.
- **Simulated pressure ("sim") / creeper**: the modern favorite — show a blitz look (linebackers mugging the A-gaps), then rush **only four**, but not the four the protection expects. A linebacker rushes, an edge drops off, and the offense's protection math is wrong even though the coverage behind it is full-strength. Sims are how defenses like Brian Flores's in Minnesota generate pressure-level disruption while keeping seven in coverage — chaos at a discount.

The protection chess behind this: offenses set their protection by declaring a "MIKE point" — five linemen can block four down linemen plus the identified MIKE. Mugged linebackers, late safety walks, and sims all exist to make that declaration wrong. When you see a free rusher hit a QB untouched, it's rarely a lineman getting beat; it's the math getting beat.

## Putting it together

A snap of defense, read in order: front structure (odd or even, who's over the center), box count (light six, standard seven, loaded eight), shell (one-high or two-high), leverage tells at corner, then the post-snap rotation that reveals the truth. Offenses are running the same checklist and calling plays to attack the answer — last chapter's concept-vs-coverage table is the other half of this one.

In the data, coverage identity shows up indirectly but reliably: blitz-heavy staffs, two-high-leaning staffs, and man-heavy staffs have distinct statistical fingerprints (pressure rate, explosive-pass rate allowed, EPA/play by down). The [Coaches](/coaches) page tracks defensive tendencies by staff, and the scheme-family chapters that follow trace how these ideas — Seattle's Cover 3, Fangio's two-high, Belichick's man-match — took over the modern league.
