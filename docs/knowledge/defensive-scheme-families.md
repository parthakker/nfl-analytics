# Defensive Scheme Families

Defensive history is a call-and-response with offense: every dominant defense is an answer to the offense that ruled the previous five years, and every offensive revolution is an answer to that answer. The modern chapter of the story runs from Seattle's single-high Cover 3 dynasty, through Vic Fangio's two-high counter-revolution that now shapes half the league, alongside Belichick's game-plan man-match school and the Flores/Ryan blitz-everything insurgency. This chapter traces each family — what it believes, why it won its era, who carries it now, and what it looks like in the numbers.

## The Seattle Cover 3 era

**The scheme.** Pete Carroll's early-2010s Seahawks played what looked like the simplest defense in football: single-high Cover 3 on a huge share of snaps, a 4-3 under front, and almost no blitzing. The sophistication was in the details — press-bail corners playing outside leverage with rare length (Richard Sherman was the prototype, and Seattle famously hunted tall, long-armed corners in the draft's later rounds), Earl Thomas erasing the deep middle with range no other safety had, Kam Chancellor as an eighth run defender, and **Rip/Liz match rules** (carrying vertical seams man-to-man, as covered in the fronts-and-coverages chapter) so four verticals couldn't cheat the math.

**Why it won.** Simplicity as a weapon: playing one structure thousands of reps deep meant no busts, fast eyes, and violent tackling. The Legion of Boom led the NFL in scoring defense four straight seasons (2012–2015), won Super Bowl XLVIII by demolishing the record-setting Broncos offense, and spawned copycats everywhere as Carroll assistants spread out: Dan Quinn (Atlanta, then Dallas's takeaway machine, now Washington's head coach — his 2024 Commanders reached the NFC title game), Gus Bradley (a half-dozen stops), Robert Saleh (via the 49ers branch, to the Jets and back to San Francisco as coordinator in 2025).

**Why it faded.** Offenses solved single-high. Condensed-split play-action offenses (the Shanahan tree) dragged Cover 3's underneath defenders with run action and threw deep overs behind them; elite slot receivers tortured the nickel with no inside help; and the deep-post/crosser combination (Yankee) turned the lone free safety's job into an unwinnable choose-one. By the late 2010s, playing 70% single-high was a losing bet against top passing games — which set the stage for the counter-revolution.

## The Fangio two-high revolution

**The scheme.** Vic Fangio — a career defensive coordinator (49ers, Bears) before becoming Broncos head coach and later coordinating Miami and the Eagles' Super Bowl LIX-winning defense of the 2024 season — built the system that defines the current era. Core tenets:

- **Two-high shells as the default picture**, heavy on quarters-match and Cover 6, with late rotations disguising everything. The QB sees the same MOF-open picture every snap and learns nothing pre-snap.
- **Light boxes on purpose.** Fangio concedes the 4-yard run to take away the 40-yard play-action shot: safeties start deep, read their run/pass keys *slowly* ("late-eyes" discipline), and rally down. The bet is that offenses lack the patience to run 12 straight times — and that a methodical 12-play drive gives you 12 chances at a drive-killing penalty, sack, or turnover.
- **Rush four, cover seven.** Fangio blitzes near the bottom of the league; pressure comes from front talent and from sims/creepers rather than numbers.
- **Positionless safeties and the nickel as a base position**, so the same personnel plays every down.

**Why it won.** It's the structural answer to the Shanahan/McVay ecosystem. Play-action works by lying to defenders' eyes; Fangio's answer is defenders whose assignments don't depend on believing the backfield. Deep overs and Yankee shots die against two deep safeties who never bit. The famous proof point: the 2021 season saw defenses league-wide surge to two-high shells against the Rams and their imitators, and explosive pass rates dropped league-wide in the seasons that followed as the shell spread.

**The tree.** Brandon Staley (Fangio's outside-linebackers coach, whose 2020 Rams defense led the league and won him the Chargers head job), DeMeco Ryans (the 49ers' Fangio-influenced defense, then Houston's head coach with top-tier defenses in 2023–24), Steve Wilks, Jesse Minter (whose 2024 Chargers defense under Jim Harbaugh allowed the fewest points in football), Joe Barry, Raheem Morris, Sean Desai, and more. By the mid-2020s, "Fangio-style" describes something like half of NFL defenses, and Fangio himself won a ring calling the Eagles' defense in the 2024 season — the ultimate validation lap. The offensive counter-move is already underway: heavier personnel, gap-scheme downhill running, and QB run games designed to punish light boxes until the safeties come down. Call and response, forever.

## Belichick: man-match and the game-plan defense

**The scheme.** Bill Belichick's school (Giants LB coach/DC under Parcells, Browns head coach, then two decades of Patriots titles) is less a fixed structure than a **method**: identify what the opponent does best, take it away, and force their third-best player to beat you. One week that's double-teaming the X receiver with bracket coverage; the next it's playing 6-1 fronts against outside zone or dropping eight into an amoeba zone against a rhythm passer. The famous examples are game plans: the 2001 season's Super Bowl win hitting the Rams' receivers at every level, and Super Bowl LIII holding McVay's Rams to 3 points with a two-high, quarters-heavy plan the Patriots had barely shown all season (a game plan that, in football-history terms, prefigured the league's whole two-high pivot).

**The DNA.** Man-match principles — corners who travel with receivers, safeties who bracket, linebackers taught coverage leverage rather than zone landmarks; an obsession with situational football (red zone, two-minute, third down get their own game plans); versatile chess pieces valued over one-trick athletes; and volume of adjustments as the core weapon. It demands enormous preparation from players, which is why it travels poorly with thin rosters — the tree (Matt Patricia, Brian Flores as a partial branch, Joe Judge, Jerod Mayo, Mike Vrabel through the Pittsburgh/Belichick lineage blend) has a mixed record without Belichick-grade infrastructure. Vrabel's Titans and now Patriots teams (he took over New England in 2025) carry the situational-football DNA most successfully.

## Pressure school: Flores, the Ryans, and the zero-blitz philosophy

**The lineage.** Buddy Ryan's 46 defense (the 1985 Bears, still the standard for single-season defensive dominance) established the creed: **the quarterback is the problem; hit him until the problem stops**. Sons Rex and Rob Ryan carried the overload-blitz, exotic-front torch; Dick LeBeau's fire-zone Steelers institutionalized the zone blitz; Wade Phillips ran one-gap attacking 3-4s to a Super Bowl 50 title with Denver's historic pass rush; Jim Johnson's Eagles blitz packages begat Steve Spagnuolo, whose Chiefs defenses won multiple Super Bowls with perfectly timed pressure (his Super Bowl LVII and LVIII game plans were blitz-timing masterclasses, capped by the game-sealing pressures against San Francisco).

**The modern face: Brian Flores.** Flores (a Belichick pressure-and-man hybrid) turned Miami and then Minnesota (DC from 2023) into the league's most aggressive defense: league-leading blitz rates, **Cover 0 looks on early downs**, all six box defenders "mugging" the line pre-snap so the offense cannot identify who's rushing, and a rotation of sims behind it. The 2024 Vikings rode it to 14 wins. The philosophy of the zero blitz: modern offenses are built on rhythm, protection math, and pre-snap certainty — so destroy the certainty. Send more rushers than blockers, force the hot throw, and trust press-man corners for the 2.3 seconds the play lasts.

**The trade-off.** Pressure defense is variance-seeking: it produces sacks, turnovers, and free rushers, and it also produces the occasional 70-yard touchdown when the blitz doesn't get home. Fangio ball is the variance-minimizing opposite. Neither is "right" — the choice tracks roster (do you have corners who can live alone?) and coordinator temperament. For bettors this matters directly: blitz-heavy defenses fatten both tails of the scoring distribution, which is worth remembering when a total looks suspiciously makeable on the [Betting](/betting) board.

## How modern defenses answer wide zone + play-action

Since the Shanahan ecosystem is the offense to beat, the counters are worth naming as a set:

1. **Two-high, light box, late eyes** (Fangio): remove the shot plays, rally to the run, dare the offense to stay patient.
2. **Tite/odd fronts with 4i ends**: wall the B-gaps so wide zone can't cut back, spilling everything to the sideline where speed cleans up.
3. **Edge-setting as religion**: outside zone and boots both die when the force defender refuses to be reached; teams now draft edge players partly on their ability to squeeze the zone track.
4. **Sims instead of blitzes**: play-action punishes blitzing linebackers, so modern defenses pressure with four-man games and creepers that don't vacate the intermediate zones where overs and digs live.
5. **Run-through "green dog" rules in man**: if your man blocks, add to the rush — a Flores-school way to punish play-action's seven-man protections.

## What shows up in the data

Scheme identity leaves statistical fingerprints, and the staff pages on [Coaches](/coaches) surface several of them:

| Signal | What it tells you |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Blitz rate** | The cleanest school separator: Flores/Spagnuolo-family defenses live at the top of the league; Fangio-family at the bottom. Stable within a coordinator across teams — it's philosophy, not personnel. |
| **Two-high rate** (pre-snap shell) | The Fangio-tree membership card. Watch it spike when a new tree coordinator arrives. |
| **Man vs. zone rate** | Belichick/Flores branches lean man; Seattle-tree and Fangio-tree lean zone/match. |
| **Pressure rate vs. blitz rate gap** | Defenses that pressure a lot while blitzing little (the Fangio ideal) have elite fronts or elite sim design; blitzing a lot without pressure is the death profile. |
| **EPA/play allowed, split by pass and run** | Fangio-style defenses often look mediocre against the run and excellent against explosive passes — by design. Judging them on run-stop rate misses the bet they're making. |
| **Explosive pass rate allowed** | The two-high shell's whole purpose; the metric it should move first. |
| **Variance of points allowed** | Pressure defenses swing week to week; shell defenses stabilize. Useful for totals and live betting alike. |

A practical reading habit: when a defense changes coordinators, check blitz rate and two-high rate after three or four weeks — those tendencies move immediately with the scheme, long before results stabilize, and markets are often slow to reprice a defense whose *structure* changed overnight. Pair the tendency shift with EPA-allowed trends (queryable per team-season in this app's warehouse, and summarized on [Coaches](/coaches)) and you'll classify every defense in the league within a month of kickoff.

## The next turn of the wheel

The pattern to bet on: offenses are already countering the two-high era with heavy personnel, downhill gap runs, QB run games, and condensed formations that force safeties into run fits — and the defensive response (bigger nickels, hybrid box safeties like Kyle Hamilton, fronts that can two-gap from light boxes) is half-built. Whoever solves "be sound against power football without giving back the deep middle" wins the next five years, and some position coach nobody has heard of yet is currently sketching it. That's the fun of the call-and-response: this chapter will need a new section every three seasons, and the tendency data will spot the shift before the highlight shows do.
