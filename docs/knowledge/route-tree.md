# The Route Tree & Passing Concepts

Every NFL passing game, no matter how exotic it looks on Sunday, is assembled from about a dozen basic routes and a few dozen ways of combining them. The routes are the alphabet; the combinations — "concepts" — are the words. Once you can name both, football broadcasts change: you stop seeing 22 players scattering and start seeing a smash concept stressing a Cover 2 corner, or four verticals splitting a single-high safety. This chapter covers the numbered route tree, the routes that live outside the numbers, and the core concepts every NFL offense carries — including which coverage each one is designed to beat.

## The route tree: 0–9

The classic route tree assigns numbers to routes branching off a single vertical "stem." The convention: **even numbers break inside, odd numbers break outside** (some teams flip it, but this is the common version). Depths below are typical NFL depths; every team adjusts them by a yard or two.

![The numbered route tree](/knowledge/route-tree.svg)

| # | Route | Break | Typical depth | The job |
|---|-------|-------|---------------|---------|
| 0/1 | Flat / Quick out | Outside | 1–3 yds | Immediate width; blitz answer; stretches flat defenders |
| 2 | Slant | Inside | 1–3 yds | Three steps, break inside on a diagonal; the highest-volume route in football |
| 3 | Comeback | Outside, back downhill | 12–15, back to 10–12 | Beat off-coverage; sideline timing throw before the corner can drive |
| 4 | Curl (hook) | Inside, back downhill | 10–12, settle | Sit down in the window between zones; chain-mover |
| 5 | Out | Outside, flat | 10–12 | Hard 90° break to the sideline; a velocity test for the QB |
| 6 | In / Dig | Inside, flat | 12–15 | Hard 90° break across the field; the money route vs. zone middle |
| 7 | Corner (flag) | Outside, deep | 10–12, break at 45° | Attack the deep-outside void behind a flat-footed corner |
| 8 | Post | Inside, deep | 10–12, break at 45° | Attack the deep middle; the route that punishes single-high safeties |
| 9 | Go / Fade | None | Vertical | Pure speed down the sideline; also the back-shoulder and red-zone fade family |

A few things the table can't show:

- **The stem is the lie.** Every route in the tree starts with the same vertical release, which is the whole point: the corner can't know at 5 yards whether he's defending a slant or a go. Great route-runners (Davante Adams is the perennial example) make every stem identical until the instant of the break.
- **Depth is coverage-dependent.** A "12-yard dig" becomes a 10-yard dig against pressure, a 14-yard dig against soft zone. NFL routes are frameworks, not train tracks.
- **The comeback and curl are timing routes.** The ball is often thrown before the receiver breaks. This is why quarterback-receiver reps matter: the QB is throwing to a spot the receiver hasn't reached yet.

## Routes that live off the tree

The numbered tree covers the outside-receiver skeleton. The rest of the passing game:

| Route | What it is |
|-------|------------|
| Screen (WR/RB/TE) | The ball thrown behind or at the line with blockers set up in front — bubble, tunnel, slip, and the classic RB screen. Screens are a run-game cousin: designed yards after catch. |
| Shallow cross | A sprint across the field at 1–4 yards; a man-coverage killer because the defender must chase through traffic |
| Deep cross / Over | A crosser that climbs to 15–20 yards — the signature route of the Shanahan/McVay play-action game |
| Wheel | Flat route that turns up the sideline; ambushes linebackers and safeties who jump the flat |
| Seam | A vertical from the slot or TE, splitting zone landmarks; bends away from the deep safety |
| Option route | Receiver reads the defender and breaks accordingly — sit vs. zone, break away vs. man. The domain of trusted slot receivers and receiving backs |
| Angle (Texas) | RB route that fakes to the flat, then breaks sharply inside — brutal against man-covering linebackers |
| Sluggo / hitch-and-go | Double moves: sell the short route, then go. Tendency-breakers that cash in on a corner's film study |
| Pivot / return | Break one way, snap back the other — a two-way-go route from the slot |

## From routes to concepts

A **concept** is two or more routes designed to work together against a specific coverage structure — usually by putting one defender in a bind where both of his choices are wrong. NFL passing games are mostly half-field concepts (a two- or three-man combination to one side, with a complementary tag on the other) plus a handful of full-field designs. Here are the ones you'll see every single week.

### Quick game and zone-stretchers

**Stick.** Three receivers to one side: the outside runs a clear-out or hitch, the #3 (innermost) runs a 5–6 yard option that "sticks" in the void, and someone runs to the flat. The read is the flat defender: if he widens with the flat, throw the stick; if he sits on the stick, throw the flat. Simple, nearly unstoppable on 3rd-and-4, and a staple of every NFL offense.

**Spot (snag).** A triangle: corner route over the top, a "spot" route snagging at 5–6 yards inside, and a flat route underneath. Triangles are beautiful because they give the QB a high-low *and* an in-out stretch simultaneously — some defender in that triangle is wrong against both man and zone.

**Slant-flat.** The slant behind, the flat in front; read the overhang defender. If he expands to the flat, the slant window opens. Two-step drop, ball out. The blitz answer every offense carries.

### High-low concepts (vertical stretches)

**Smash.** The classic Cover 2 beater. Outside receiver runs a hitch (or short smash route); slot runs a corner route behind him. The Cover 2 cornerback, responsible for the flat, is high-lowed: sink under the corner route and the hitch is free; squat on the hitch and the corner route drops in behind him, in front of the deep-half safety. One defender, two jobs, zero good options.

**Levels.** Two in-breaking routes at different depths — a shallow in at ~5 and a dig at ~12 — high-lowing the inside linebacker in zone. Peyton Manning's Colts made levels famous; every team runs it now.

**Drive.** A shallow cross from one side with a dig coming from the other side behind it, usually with a checkdown as the third layer. Same high-low idea as levels, but the crosser sprinting through traffic makes it strong against man too. A Shanahan-tree staple.

**Mesh.** The Air Raid's signature: two shallow crossers from opposite sides "mesh" a yard apart in the middle of the field (traditionally, one sets the depth and the other rubs just underneath). Against man, the crossers scrape their trailing defenders off on each other and someone runs free. Against zone, both crossers settle in windows. Modern NFL versions (Mesh Rail) add a wheel/rail route from the back to punish defenses that sink under the crossers. Kliff Kingsbury, Sean McVay, and half the league run some version of it.

**Shallow cross (Hi-Lo shallow).** The single-crosser cousin of mesh: one shallow, a dig behind it, often a post over the top. The read is a clean triangle progression, and it beats man (shallow wins by legs) and zone (dig sits behind the second-level defenders).

### Field-stretchers and coverage-splitters

**Four verticals.** Four receivers up the field on landmarks: outside receivers up the numbers, inside receivers up the seams — with the seam runners bending away from safety leverage. Against Cover 3 (one deep-middle safety), four verts is a 4-on-3 arithmetic problem: the free safety cannot cover both seams, and the QB throws the one he vacates. Against Cover 2, the outside verticals hold the safeties and the seams split them. Every "bender" completion up the middle you've ever seen from Justin Herbert or Josh Allen is this math.

**Dagger.** A seam route from the slot clears out the deep middle; the outside receiver runs a deep dig into the vacated grass. Devastating against single-high zone (Cover 3): the seam drags the free safety and the hook defenders sink with it, and the dig crosses into an empty MOF window. One of the most-copied concepts of the McVay era.

**Flood (sail).** Three levels to one sideline: a go/clear-out deep, a sail/corner route at 15–18, and a flat route underneath. It out-numbers the two or three zone defenders on that side — someone is always uncovered vertically. Almost always paired with a rollout or play-action to that side, which also cuts the field in half for a young QB. The natural Cover 3 stressor, since Cover 3 has one curl-flat defender per side trying to cover two of those layers.

**Post-cross (Yankee).** Two-man deep shot off heavy play-action: a deep post from one side, a deep over/crosser from the other, splitting a single deep safety who cannot take both. Maximum protection, seven or eight blockers, one binary read. This is the shot play of the wide-zone world — the Rams under McVay with Cooper Kupp and the Robert Woods-era over routes, the 49ers with Deebo Samuel, the Vikings' deep game with Justin Jefferson all lean on it.

### Play-action's role

Play-action isn't a concept, it's a multiplier. The fake handoff freezes second-level defenders for a beat — linebackers step downhill, safeties hesitate — and the intermediate windows (digs, overs, posts) get wider. Two facts worth internalizing, both robust across seasons of league-wide data: play-action passes are meaningfully more efficient than straight dropbacks, and the effect **does not depend on running well or often first** — defenders react to the fake regardless, because their run keys (linemen firing out, backfield flow) are the same. That's why analysts treat low play-action rates as a coaching failure rather than a personnel constraint, and why play-action rate is a core tendency stat on the [Coaches](/coaches) page.

## Beating coverages: the cheat sheet

Concepts exist to attack structures. The pairings, compressed:

| Coverage | Structural weakness | Concepts that attack it |
|----------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Cover 0/1 (man) | No help; defenders chase through traffic | Mesh, shallow cross, rubs/stacks, option routes, slants, back-shoulder fades |
| Cover 2 | Corner high-lowed in flat; deep middle open between safeties | Smash, four verts (split the safeties), seam/post from #2 |
| Cover 3 | One curl-flat defender per side; seams stress one FS | Flood/sail, four verts, dagger, deep overs |
| Cover 4 (quarters) | Flats undefended early; safeties bite on run action | Quick outs and flats, levels, post-cross play-action shots |
| Cover 6 | Different weakness each side | Attack the quarter side like C4, the half side like C2 |

Two closing habits for watching (or charting) the passing game. First, count the layers to one side after the snap — three receivers at three depths on one sideline is flood; two crossers meeting in the middle is mesh; hitch-under-corner is smash. Second, watch the deep middle of the field: whether it's occupied (one safety) or open (two safeties) tells you which half of each concept was the "live" side. That's exactly the read the QB made a half-second before the throw — and the next chapter, on fronts and coverages, shows the defense's side of that chess match.
