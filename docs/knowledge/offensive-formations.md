# Offensive Formations & Personnel

Before the ball is snapped, an NFL offense has already told you a lot — if you know how to read it. Who's on the field (personnel), how they're arranged (formation), and who's moving (motion) are the three pre-snap layers every broadcast analyst, defensive coordinator, and sharp bettor is processing in the same five seconds. This chapter gives you the vocabulary and the logic: what "11 personnel" means, why it swallowed the league, what an I-formation still buys you in 2025, and why a receiver jogging across the formation before the snap is one of the most information-dense events in football.

## Personnel groupings: the two-digit code

NFL personnel is described with a two-digit number: **first digit = running backs, second digit = tight ends**. Wide receivers are whatever's left over, because there are always five eligible skill players (11 players, minus the QB and five linemen).

| Code | RB | TE | WR | Common name | Typical use |
|------|----|----|----|-------------|-------------|
| 11 | 1 | 1 | 3 | "Eleven" / posse | The default NFL grouping — spread the field, keep a back for protection and checkdowns |
| 12 | 1 | 2 | 2 | "Twelve" / ace | Two-TE flexibility; run-heavy look that can still spread out |
| 21 | 2 | 1 | 2 | "Twenty-one" / pro | Fullback football; the Shanahan-tree favorite |
| 13 | 1 | 3 | 1 | "Thirteen" | Heavy but sneaky-versatile if the TEs can catch |
| 10 | 1 | 0 | 4 | "Ten" | Four-wide spread; pass-first, two-minute, obvious passing downs |
| 22 | 2 | 2 | 1 | "Twenty-two" | Short yardage, goal line, clock-killing |
| 00 | 0 | 0 | 5 | Empty personnel | Five receivers, no back — rare as a *grouping* (empty is usually a formation from 11) |
| 23 / 6 OL | 2 | 3 | 0 | "Jumbo" / goal line | Extra linemen often report eligible; sledgehammer football |

Two clarifications that trip people up:

- **Personnel is who's on the field, not where they line up.** An offense can be in 12 personnel and split both tight ends out wide into an empty set. This is exactly the point of versatile TEs: same bodies, wildly different pictures.
- **A "jumbo" package** often includes a sixth offensive lineman reporting as eligible. When you hear the referee announce a number reporting, heavy football is coming.

## Why 11 personnel took over the league

Across the NFL, 11 personnel has been the majority grouping for roughly a decade — league-wide usage has hovered around 60% of snaps, with some teams (peak-era Bengals with Ja'Marr Chase, Tee Higgins, and Tyler Boyd; the Rams for most of the McVay era) living in it on 80–90% of plays. Why did one grouping win?

1. **It forces the defense's hand.** Three receivers means the defense almost always answers with nickel (five defensive backs). If they stay in base defense, you have a receiver on a linebacker or safety — an automatic win. If they go nickel, the offense has a lighter box to run against.
2. **It's the most balanced lie.** From 11, you can run outside zone, throw play-action, spread to empty, or motion into a two-back look with the TE offset. Defenses can't lean run or pass from personnel alone.
3. **Passing is more efficient than running,** and 11 is the best passing grouping that doesn't tell the defense you're passing. 10 personnel screams pass; 11 whispers "maybe."

The counter-trend is real, though. The Shanahan tree (49ers, and their many descendants) built an ecosystem around **21 personnel** with a true fullback (Kyle Juszczyk was the archetype for years), and teams with two legitimate receiving tight ends — think Baltimore, or the Patriots' old Gronkowski/Hernandez blueprint — weaponize **12 personnel** because it's genuinely two-faced: heavy enough to run at a light box, skilled enough to shred base defense with seam routes. When you browse team tendencies on the [Coaches](/coaches) page, personnel identity is one of the fastest ways to tell scheme families apart.

## Formation families

Formation is the arrangement of those bodies. A few families cover almost everything you'll see.

### Singleback

One back behind (or beside) the quarterback, no fullback. The workhorse alignment of modern football — it pairs with any personnel from 11 to 13. The QB can be under center (best for outside zone and play-action, since the back gets a downhill start and the QB's turned back sells run) or in the gun.

### I-formation and offset backs

Quarterback under center, fullback ahead of the tailback in a straight line — the classic look of 1990s football. The full "I" is now a situational tool, but **offset** variants (fullback shaded to one side, called "strong" or "weak") are alive and well in Shanahan-tree offenses. The fullback's value is as a moving gap: the defense can't know which hole he'll lead through until after the snap. It also creates maybe the league's best play-action, because the run picture is so credible.

### Shotgun and pistol

**Shotgun**: QB about five yards deep, back beside him. Best sightlines for the passing game, natural fit for RPOs and QB runs, but the back's alignment tips run direction slightly (most gun runs go away from the back's side, though good offenses break this tendency on purpose). **Pistol**: QB at about four yards with the back directly *behind* him — a compromise invented by Chris Ault at Nevada that keeps the downhill run game and hides run direction while preserving gun sightlines. Baltimore's offense with Lamar Jackson has made the pistol a core NFL alignment rather than a college curiosity.

### Empty

No back next to the QB — five receivers spread across the field, usually 3×2. Empty is a formation, not a personnel grouping: teams often get there from 11 or 12 by splitting the back and TE out. What it buys: five immediate route threats, a clean pre-snap coverage picture (defenses have to declare), and quick-game answers. What it costs: five-man protection only, so any blitz math the defense wins is on the QB's internal clock. Patrick Mahomes in empty has been one of the most efficient situations in football for years precisely because he processes the declared picture faster than the rush arrives.

### Bunch and stack

**Bunch**: three receivers clustered within a couple of yards of each other. **Stack**: two receivers aligned one directly behind the other. Both exist to break man coverage — defenders can't press a receiver who has a teammate as a shield, and the traffic creates natural rubs (legal picks, as long as contact happens within a yard of the line or is incidental). Bunch also compresses the coverage, opening the wide side of the field for the back or a crosser.

### Condensed splits

Receivers aligned tight to the formation instead of near the sidelines — a McVay-era signature. Why squeeze the field? Three reasons: receivers get **two-way gos** (room to break either inside or outside, which beats man); tight alignments put receivers in position to crack-block safeties on outside runs, making run and pass look identical; and crossing routes get a running start into open grass. When you see receivers barely outside the tackles, you're usually watching a wide-zone, play-action offense.

## Tight end attachment and nub formations

Where the tight end lines up changes the formation's math:

- **Attached (in-line)**: TE next to the tackle, creating an extra gap the defense must account for. This sets the "strong side" of most formations.
- **Flexed/split**: TE in the slot or wide. Now he's a coverage question — does a linebacker follow him out (offense wins the matchup) or does a DB take him (lighter box to run against)?
- **Wing/sniffer**: TE off the ball, tucked behind the tackle or in the backfield. Sniffer TEs are the engine of modern split-zone and play-action — they arc across the formation at the snap, insulting the defense's gap count.

A **nub formation** puts a tight end as the *only* player on one side — no receiver outside him. This creates a short "closed" side and forces uncomfortable choices on the defense: a cornerback has to align over a blocking tight end (wasting a cover player, and inviting the offense to run right at him) or the defense rotates its structure and reveals its coverage. Nub sets are a favorite tool of run-first teams and a classic coverage-identification trick.

## Pre-snap motion: the free information machine

Motion — one player relocating before the snap — is legal as long as only one player moves at the snap and he isn't moving toward the line of scrimmage (that's the NFL rule; unlike college, a receiver can be moving laterally or backward at the snap). Analytically, motion is one of the best-documented "free money" behaviors in football: snaps with motion, especially motion *at* the snap, have consistently graded out as more efficient league-wide, and the highest-motion offenses of recent seasons (Miami under McDaniel, San Francisco, the Rams, Baltimore) are a who's-who of good offenses. Motion rate is one of the tendency stats surfaced for each staff on the [Coaches](/coaches) page.

What motion does:

### 1. It's a coverage identification test

The oldest trick in the book: send a receiver across the formation and watch the defense.

- **A defender chases him across** → almost certainly **man coverage**.
- **The defense slides or bumps zones, nobody travels** → almost certainly **zone**.

Defenses know offenses know this, so they'll sometimes travel in zone or bump in man to lie — but the tell is still right far more often than not, and quarterbacks build their pre-snap plan around it.

### 2. It changes the math after the snap

- **Jet motion**: a receiver sprints full-speed across the formation, timed to cross the QB at the snap. Even when he doesn't get the ball, the defense must honor the jet sweep, holding a defender on the edge and freezing linebacker flow. When he does get it, he's receiving a handoff at top speed against a defense that has to change direction.
- **Orbit/return motion**: the player loops behind the QB and comes back the way he came, or arcs into the flat as a pitch/screen threat. Great for holding backside defenders on zone-read-style actions.
- **Shift + reset**: not technically motion, but related — the whole strength of the formation moves (TE trades sides, backs swap), forcing the defense to re-declare its strength calls seconds before the snap. Tired defenses miscommunicate; offenses feast on the miscommunication.

### 3. It creates leverage that can't be answered

Motion at the snap gives the moving player a head start on a stationary defender. A receiver motioning into a stack or bunch right before the snap gets a free release against press. A back motioning wide either drags a linebacker into space (mismatch) or reveals a zone shell (information). There is no clean defensive answer, which is why motion usage has climbed league-wide every season and why low-motion offenses increasingly look like a scheme choice from a previous decade.

## Reading it all together

Here's the pre-snap checklist, the same one a MIKE linebacker runs through in reverse:

1. **Personnel**: who ran onto the field? 11 tells you little (that's the point); 13 or 22 narrows the playbook a lot.
2. **Formation**: under center with a sniffer TE and condensed splits leans run/play-action; gun empty declares pass.
3. **Alignment details**: nub side, TE attached or flexed, back's depth and side.
4. **Motion**: what did the defense's answer reveal?
5. **Situation**: down, distance, and clock override everything — 12 personnel on 3rd-and-9 is still probably a pass.

None of these tells is certain alone; together, they're most of the battle. The best play-callers — study the tendency profiles on the [Coaches](/coaches) page — are the ones who deliberately break their own patterns: running from 10 personnel, throwing deep from 22, using the same motion for four different plays. When you can't predict a team from its pre-snap picture, that's not a lack of identity. That's usually the identity.
