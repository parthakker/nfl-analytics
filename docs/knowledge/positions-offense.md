# Offensive Positions

Eleven players, five of whom are ineligible to catch a pass, all organized around getting one ball 10 yards at a time. The labels — QB, RB, WR, TE, OL — are the easy part. What each position actually *does*, how the roles have mutated over the last two decades, and what separates good from great at each spot is where it gets interesting.

![Base 11-personnel offensive alignment](/knowledge/offense-alignment.svg)

## The Alignment Rules First

Two rules govern every legal offensive formation:

1. **Seven players must be on the line of scrimmage** at the snap.
2. Only the players at each **end** of that line, plus the four players off the line, are eligible receivers.

That's why receivers care about being "on" or "off" the ball: an outside receiver on the line (the X) makes the tight end next to the tackle *covered* and ineligible if the TE is also on the line. Formations are a legality puzzle before they're a strategy.

| Position | Typical alignment | On the line? |
|---|---|---|
| QB | Under center or shotgun (4–5 yds deep) | No |
| RB | Beside/behind the QB | No |
| X receiver (split end) | Wide, usually weak side | **Yes** |
| Z receiver (flanker) | Wide, strong side, off the ball | No |
| Slot receiver | Between the X/Z and the line, off the ball | No |
| TE (Y) | Attached next to a tackle, or flexed | Usually yes |
| OL: LT, LG, C, RG, RT | Shoulder to shoulder around the ball | Yes |

## Quarterback

The most important position in American team sports, and it isn't close. The QB touches the ball on every offensive snap, makes or adjusts the protection call, reads the defense before and after the snap, and delivers the ball under duress. Quarterback play explains more of the variance in team success than any other single input — which is why QB injuries move point spreads by multiple points while a star at any other position moves them a fraction of that.

**What separates good from great:** processing speed and accuracy under pressure. Arm strength is the trait fans see; the traits that actually sort the tiers are (a) how fast the picture resolves post-snap, (b) ball placement — not just completions, but *catchable-and-in-stride* placement that creates yards after catch, and (c) what happens when the play dies. The 2020s superstars — Patrick Mahomes, Josh Allen, Lamar Jackson, Joe Burrow — are all elite at the third thing: they turn dead plays into first downs, either with their legs or with off-platform throws that weren't in the playbook.

**The modern shift:** the position absorbed the running game. Designed QB runs and scrambles went from a gimmick to a core efficiency engine — a QB who can credibly run adds a blocker's worth of math to every run play (the defense must account for 11 threats, not 10). Lamar Jackson's MVP seasons and Jalen Hurts's "tush push" short-yardage dominance are the headline examples. Meanwhile, pure statue pocket passers still exist but must be *elite* processors to justify the tradeoff.

Track weekly QB production on the [Leaders](/leaders) page — and note how the top of the EPA-per-play leaderboard is almost always QBs on rosters that make the playoffs. That's not coincidence; that's the position's leverage.

## Running Back / Fullback

**Running back** is football's cruelest position economically: maximum physical toll, shortest careers, and an analytics consensus that rushing efficiency is driven more by blocking and box counts than by the runner. The franchise-tag-era standoffs of the early 2020s (Saquon Barkley, Josh Jacobs, Jonathan Taylor all hitting contract friction in the same offseason) were the market catching up to the data.

And yet — the position isn't dead, it's *bifurcated*. Backs who only run between the tackles are replaceable. Backs who create explosive plays and function as real receivers are offensive centerpieces: Christian McCaffrey's 2023 (Offensive Player of the Year as the engine of the 49ers offense) and Saquon Barkley's monster 2024 in Philadelphia — including his 2,000-yard regular season on the way to a Super Bowl — showed what the ceiling still looks like when a great back meets a great blocking infrastructure.

**What separates good from great:** vision and tempo (knowing when to press the hole and when to bounce), contact balance, and — increasingly the dividing line — receiving chops. Third-down backs used to be specialists; now the *starter* is expected to run routes from the slot.

**Fullback** is nearly extinct as a full-time job — most teams carry zero or use one for a handful of snaps. The survivors (Kyle Juszczyk in San Francisco being the archetype) exist because Shanahan-tree offenses use them as moving puzzle pieces: lead blocker, arc blocker, occasional leak-out receiver. When you see "21 personnel" in a charting table, a fullback (or an H-back doing a fullback's job) is usually why.

## Wide Receiver: X, Z, and Slot

"Wide receiver" is three different jobs wearing one label.

| Role | Alignment | Job description | Body-type stereotype |
|---|---|---|---|
| **X (split end)** | On the line, usually solo on the weak side | Beat press coverage alone, win outside | Big, strong, contested-catch |
| **Z (flanker)** | Off the line, strong side | Motion, free releases, timing routes | Fluid, faster |
| **Slot** | Inside, off the line | Work the middle, beat nickels, option routes | Quick, sudden, tough |

The **X** has the hardest life: because he's on the line of scrimmage, he can't motion and gets pressed by the opponent's best corner with no free release, no pick help, no cushion. That's why "true X" is scouting shorthand for a receiver who can win one-on-one outside — the Ja'Marr Chase / Mike Evans job.

The **Z** aligns off the ball, which unlocks motion and stacked/bunched releases. Coordinators put their timing-and-route-craft receiver here.

The **slot** used to be where small possession guys hid. The modern slot is a featured weapon: interior alignments create two-way gos against off coverage and matchup leverage against nickel corners and safeties. The revolution was realizing your *best* receiver can live there — Tyreek Hill and CeeDee Lamb take huge snap shares inside, and Cooper Kupp's 2021 triple-crown season was built largely from the slot. Alignment versatility itself became the trait: Justin Jefferson lines up everywhere precisely so defenses can't script a plan for him.

**What separates good from great:** separation is the skill; everything else is decoration. Great receivers win at the release, at the top of the route, and at the catch point — three separate battles. The elite tier (Jefferson, Chase, Hill in his prime) wins at least two of them on nearly every snap. Yards after catch is the other modern separator — a slant caught in stride against a beaten nickel is a 60-yard play for Hill and an 8-yard play for a lesser athlete, and offenses are explicitly designed around that difference.

## Tight End: Inline and Move

The tight end is football's duality experiment: part offensive lineman, part receiver, and the position splits into two roles:

- **Inline (Y):** hand in the dirt next to the tackle, blocking on run downs, releasing into routes off play-action. A true inline TE who blocks *and* catches is maybe the scarcest non-QB commodity in the sport — George Kittle is the modern gold standard, a legitimate sixth lineman who also breaks tackles like a running back.
- **Move (F / "big slot"):** flexed into the slot or backfield, motioned across the formation, hunted against linebackers and safeties. Travis Kelce built a Hall of Fame career here — Kansas City's offense for a decade was substantially "Kelce option routes vs. whoever you've got." The younger wave — Sam LaPorta, Brock Bowers (who broke rookie TE receiving records in 2024), Trey McBride — are used this way from day one.

Why defenses hate good tight ends: they break the personnel logic. A TE who blocks well *and* runs routes well means the same 12-personnel grouping can be a power run or a four-receiver spread — the defense must pick base or nickel before the offense shows its hand. That's the entire strategic point of the position in the 2020s.

**What separates good from great:** the blocking floor. Plenty of TEs can run a seam route; the ones who force base defenses onto the field and then torch them are the difference-makers.

## The Offensive Line: C, G, T — and the Blindside

Five players, one organism. The line is the least-statted, most-important unit in football — pressure rate allowed and run-block win rate move a team's entire offensive profile, and no skill player outruns bad protection for long.

| Spot | Job emphasis | What great looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Center (C)** | Snaps + makes the protection calls; the line's QB | Instant ID of fronts and blitzes; anchor vs. nose tackles — think Jason Kelce or Creed Humphrey |
| **Guards (LG/RG)** | Interior power, pulling on gap schemes, stopping interior rush | Quenton Nelson-style people-moving plus the feet to handle 3-techs like Chris Jones |
| **Tackles (LT/RT)** | Island pass protection vs. EDGE rushers | Trent Williams, Penei Sewell: mirror elite speed, anchor elite power, alone in space |

The **blindside** — the left tackle for a right-handed QB — became famous as the second-highest-paid position in football because a QB can't brace for a hit he never sees. That premium has flattened somewhat: right tackles now face teams' best rushers regularly (defenses hunt matchups, not sides), and pass rushes attack the *interior* more than ever because pressure up the middle arrives faster and denies the QB a stepping-up escape. Guards and centers who can't pass-protect are now unhideable.

**The modern shift:** OL play became pass-protection-first, and scheme fit rules everything. Zone-blocking teams (the Shanahan tree) draft lighter, athletic movers; gap/power teams (Philadelphia, Detroit's identity under their mauling line) want mass and nastiness. The Eagles' and Lions' runs of success in 2023–24 were both, at root, offensive line stories — as was Barkley's 2,000-yard season the moment he stepped behind one of them.

## How It All Fits

Watch any snap and run the checklist: Is the TE attached or flexed? Is the back offset or beside the QB in the gun? Who's the isolated X? Where's the slot? You'll start seeing formations as *questions the offense asks the defense* — and personnel packages as the vocabulary.
