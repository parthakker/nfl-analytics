# How the League Works

The NFL on the field is 22 players and a ball; the NFL as an institution is a year-round machine of deadlines, allocation rules, and money mechanics — and almost every roster decision you see reported makes sense only inside that machine. Why did a team cut a good player in March? Cap acceleration. Why did a contender lose a starter and get a 3rd-round pick a year later? Compensatory formula. Why does a 9-8 team host an 11-6 team in January? Seeding rules. This chapter is the operating manual: the calendar, the playoff format, the draft, the salary cap, and the roster plumbing underneath it all.

## The season calendar

The league year is a loop with hard deadlines. Approximate dates (they drift a few days year to year):

| Window | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Franchise/transition tag window | Mid-Feb → early March | Teams may tag one pending free agent |
| Scouting Combine | Late Feb – early March | Draft prospects tested/interviewed in Indianapolis |
| Legal tampering | ~2 days before league year | Agents may negotiate (not sign) with other teams |
| **New league year** | Mid-March | Free agency opens; trades execute; contracts hit the cap |
| The draft | Late April | Seven rounds over three days |
| Rookie minicamp / OTAs | May–June | Voluntary (mostly) offseason program; no pads |
| Mandatory minicamp | Mid-June | The one required offseason camp |
| Training camp | Late July | Rosters at 90; pads come on |
| Preseason | August | Three exhibition games; cutdown to 53 in late August |
| **Regular season** | Early Sept → early Jan | 18 weeks, 17 games, one bye per team |
| Trade deadline | Early November (week 9-ish) | Last day for in-season trades |
| Playoffs | January | 14 teams, single elimination |
| Super Bowl | Early–mid February | Ends the loop; the tag window opens days later |

A few calendar notes worth knowing. The **17th game** (added 2021) is a cross-conference matchup rotated by prior-year standings, with the extra home game alternating conferences by year. **International games** have grown into a genuine slate — London (multiple games annually), Germany, Brazil (São Paulo debuted in 2024), and Madrid joining in 2025 — and they matter analytically: they create pseudo-road games for both teams, long travel, and byes or scheduling quirks afterward. The **franchise tag** is a one-year, fully guaranteed tender at roughly the average of the top five salaries at the player's position (or 120% of his prior salary, if higher); it keeps a star off the market at the cost of cap space and, often, goodwill.

## Playoff format and seeding

Fourteen teams make the playoffs: four division winners plus three wild cards per conference, seeded 1–7.

- **Seeds 1–4** go to division winners by record — which is why a weak division's champion can host a wild card with a better record (the 2010 7-9 Seahawks hosting, and beating, the 11-5 Saints remains the canonical example).
- **Seeds 5–7** are the best non-winners by record.
- Only the **1 seed gets a bye**. Wild-card weekend is 2v7, 3v6, 4v5; after that the bracket **re-seeds** — the 1 seed always plays the worst surviving seed in the divisional round.
- Higher seed hosts every round until the Super Bowl (neutral site).

Since the seven-team field arrived in 2020, the 1 seed's bye has become enormously valuable — it's the only week off, and the math of winning two home games versus three games straight shows up plainly in conference-title outcomes.

**Tiebreakers** are a strict cascade, applied first within divisions, then for wild cards. The order for two clubs in the same division:

1. Head-to-head record
2. Division record
3. Record in common games
4. Conference record
5. Strength of victory (combined record of teams you beat)
6. Strength of schedule (combined record of all opponents)
7. …then a tail of point/ranking tiebreakers almost never reached, ending at a coin toss

Wild-card ties across different divisions swap in conference record earlier and skip division record. The details rarely matter until Week 17 — and then they're suddenly the only thing that matters. Two practical notes: head-to-head is only decisive if the sweep/record is unambiguous among *all* tied teams, and strength-of-victory scenarios are why a fan in late December can find themselves rooting hard for a team their team beat in September.

## The draft and compensatory picks

The draft is seven rounds, one pick per team per round (before trades and comp picks), ordered by reverse record — worst team picks first, Super Bowl winner picks 32nd. Non-playoff teams order by record (with strength-of-schedule as the first tiebreaker — *weaker* schedule picks earlier); playoff teams order by exit round, then record.

Structural facts that shape team-building:

- **Rookie contracts are four years**, slotted by pick with little negotiation since 2011. First-rounders carry a **fifth-year option** the team can exercise after year three. This makes draft picks the best value in football: a hit at pick 15 gives you five years of a starter at a fraction of veteran market price, which is the entire economic logic of "building through the draft."
- **Pick trades** are constant, and the old Jimmy Johnson value chart (pick 1 = 3,000 points, etc.) has been supplemented by analytics-derived charts showing teams historically overpaid to trade up. Future picks conventionally trade at roughly a one-round discount.
- **Compensatory picks**: up to 32 extra picks awarded annually at the ends of rounds 3–7, to teams that lost more/better *unrestricted* free agents than they signed the prior offseason. The formula is unpublished but well reverse-engineered (Over The Cap projects it accurately): it keys primarily on the contract's average per year, plus playtime and postseason honors. Comp picks are why disciplined teams sometimes sit out free agency's second wave — signing a mid-tier free agent can *cancel* the comp pick for a departed one. There's also a separate minority-hiring comp-pick program awarding third-rounders to teams whose executives/coaches are hired away for top jobs.

## Salary cap basics

The cap is a hard, league-wide limit on player spending per team per league year — about **$279 million in 2025**, and it rises with league revenue. Teams may carry over unused space. The interesting part isn't the number; it's the accounting.

**Cap hit** = base salary + prorated signing bonus + roster/workout/other bonuses for that year. The key mechanism is **proration**: a signing bonus is paid in cash up front but spread evenly over the contract for cap purposes, up to a **maximum of five years**. A $30M signing bonus on a five-year deal counts $6M per year.

**Dead money** is what happens when the player leaves before the prorations run out: all remaining bonus proration **accelerates** onto the current cap (if cut/traded before June 1) or splits across this year and next (with a **post–June 1 designation**, of which teams may use two per year). Dead money is money for players no longer on the team — the record books here are grim reading, headlined by Denver carrying roughly $85M in dead money across 2024–25 after releasing Russell Wilson, the largest such charge in league history at the time.

**Restructures** are the cap's pressure valve: convert base salary into signing bonus mid-contract, pushing cap dollars into future years. The player loses nothing (he gets the cash sooner, fully guaranteed); the team buys space today at the price of larger future hits and larger potential dead money. **Void years** take this further — fake contract years appended purely to spread bonus proration, which automatically "void." When the void date arrives, all that proration comes due as dead money. The Saints and Eagles turned aggressive void-year accounting into a strategy; the lesson from watching both is that the cap is *soft in any given year* but *hard over time* — every dollar is accounted for eventually, and "cap hell" is mostly just deferred spending arriving on schedule.

One more distinction that explains many headlines: **cash vs. cap**. Guarantees at signing, injury guarantees, and payment schedules are cash-flow concepts; the cap is an accounting overlay. A team "creating $20M in space" spent no less money — it moved the accounting.

## Rosters, practice squad, and IR

The roster rules are fiddly but worth knowing because they drive the transaction wire (and fantasy availability):

- **53-man roster** after final cuts; **48 may dress on game day** (46 if fewer than eight offensive linemen are active — the rule nudges teams to dress eight OL).
- **Practice squad**: 16 additional players (17 with an International Pathway player) who practice with the team but don't play. Any team may sign another club's practice-squad player — but only to its *active* roster, which is how depth gets poached. Each week teams may **elevate** up to two practice-squadders to the game-day roster; an individual player can be elevated only **three times per season** before he must be signed to the 53 to keep playing.
- **Injured reserve**: a player on IR misses at least **four games**. Teams get a limited number of **return-from-IR designations** per season (eight in recent seasons, with players placed on IR at cutdown eligible only if designated). This is why "IR, designated to return" and "IR, out for year" are different news items.
- **PUP (physically unable to perform)** and **NFI (non-football injury)** lists park players who fail their physicals at camp; opening the season on reserve/PUP also means at least four games out.
- **Waivers**: cut players with fewer than four accrued seasons pass through waivers, claimable in priority order (worst record first, same as draft order); vested veterans become free agents immediately — except after the trade deadline, when everyone passes through waivers.

## Why this machinery matters analytically

Almost every edge in evaluating teams "off the field" comes from taking this structure seriously. Rookie-contract quarterbacks let teams overspend everywhere else — the roster-construction cheat code of the modern era, visible in nearly every recent Super Bowl roster. Comp-pick discipline compounds quietly. Dead-money cliffs predict teardowns a year in advance. Division strength distorts playoff seeding, which mis-seeds home-field advantage, which betting markets price imperfectly in January. And the calendar itself creates information rhythms — March cap casualties, April draft-capital shifts, August depth-chart battles — that move markets before a single snap. The schedule, odds, and roster data behind all of this live in the warehouse this app queries; when a January seeding scenario or a March cap purge is in the news, the numbers to check it against are a query away.
