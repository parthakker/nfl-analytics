# Offensive Scheme Families

NFL offenses are not 32 independent inventions. They're branches of a handful of family trees, each descended from one coach's answer to one era's defenses — and each still visible today in a team's terminology, its favorite plays, and even its statistical fingerprint. Knowing the families turns "this offense looks different" into "this is a Shanahan-tree wide-zone offense with Air Raid quick game bolted on." This chapter walks the major lineages: where each came from, what it believes, who runs it now, and how to spot it — on film and in the data.

## West Coast: the Walsh lineage

**The idea.** Bill Walsh, coordinating for a weak-armed Virgil Carter in Cincinnati and later building the 49ers dynasty around Joe Montana, inverted football's logic: instead of running to set up deep passes, use **short, high-percentage, precisely timed passes as the run game**. Three- and five-step drops, the ball out on rhythm, receivers catching in stride so a 6-yard slant becomes a 15-yard gain. Horizontal stretches, yards after catch, and an obsessive scripting of the first 15 plays.

**The DNA markers.** Timing over arm strength; the QB's drop tied to the route's break (last step, ball out); a huge menu of crossing routes, slants, and option routes; the fullback and TE as real receiving threats. West Coast play calls are famously verbose — "Green Right Slot Spider 2 Y Banana" — because every player's assignment is spelled out in the call.

**The tree.** Walsh's assistants and their descendants have run large chunks of the league for forty years: Mike Holmgren (Favre's Packers), Andy Reid (more below), Mike Shanahan (who fused it with the wide-zone run game — see below), Jon Gruden, Sean Payton — whose Saints offense with Drew Brees was arguably the purest modern West Coast expression, all rhythm, option routes, and completion percentage. Almost every NFL offense today is at least one-quarter West Coast; the quick game and the YAC-first philosophy are simply league infrastructure now.

## Air Coryell: the vertical timing tree

**The idea.** Don Coryell's Chargers (Dan Fouts, Kellen Winslow) attacked **down the field, on timing**. Where West Coast stretches horizontally, Coryell stretches vertically: deep comebacks, posts, and seams thrown on rhythm from deeper drops, with a power run game underneath to keep safeties honest and play-action credible.

**The DNA markers.** The **number-tree terminology** — routes called by digits ("989" = go, post, go), which is where the route tree in the previous chapter became a naming system. A featured deep-threat receiver, a downhill run game, and a willingness to take 15-yard-plus shots on early downs.

**The tree.** Joe Gibbs won three Super Bowls with a run-heavy Coryell branch; Norv Turner and Ernie Zampese carried the terminology across a dozen teams (Troy Aikman's Cowboys ran it); Mike Martz's Greatest Show on Turf Rams was Coryell at maximum volume. The modern carriers: Bruce Arians's "no risk it, no biscuit" vertical offenses in Arizona and Tampa (Carson Palmer's and Tom Brady's late-career deep-ball seasons), and portions of any offense that calls routes by numbers.

## Air Raid: Leach, Mumme, and the NFL's quick-game donor

**The idea.** Hal Mumme and Mike Leach, at Kentucky and Texas Tech, distilled offense to its most repeatable throws and drilled them endlessly: **a tiny menu of concepts (mesh, four verts, Y-cross, shallow, stick) run from spread sets at high tempo, with wide line splits and total indifference to the run game**. Practice was air raid too — no call sheets thick as phone books, just mastery of ten answers that beat everything.

**The NFL adaptation.** The pure Air Raid doesn't survive NFL pass rushes (those wide splits get QBs killed), but its concepts and its QB-development model conquered the league anyway. Patrick Mahomes (Texas Tech) and the college systems that produced Joe Burrow's LSU offense, Kyler Murray's Oklahoma offense (Lincoln Riley being an Air Raid grandchild), and Jared Goff's Cal offense are all branches. Kliff Kingsbury brought it to Arizona directly. More importantly, **every NFL team now carries Air Raid staples** — mesh, four verts with a choice route, stick variations — as their third-down and two-minute package. The Air Raid won not by taking over playbooks but by donating its best organs to all of them.

**The DNA markers.** 10/11 personnel, shotgun, high pass rate on early downs, mesh and Y-cross as identity plays, QBs trained to throw to grass rather than to a script.

## Erhardt-Perkins: a terminology, not a scheme

The third great "family" is really a **naming philosophy**. Ron Erhardt and Ray Perkins's Patriots system — which Charlie Weis and then Josh McDaniels ran for Tom Brady's entire New England career — names **concepts, not routes**. One word ("Ghost," "Hoss") tells all five receivers what combination they're in, based on where they're aligned. Against West Coast's paragraph-long calls, E-P calls are one word and formation-agnostic: line up anywhere, run "Hoss" (hitches outside, seams inside), done.

**Why it matters.** Concept-based naming is what makes tempo, no-huddle, and multiplicity possible — it's why Brady could audible the entire pattern with one word at the line. Most modern offenses now blend E-P concept names with West Coast protections and Coryell numbers; when you hear coaches say an offense is "multiple," the terminology system is usually E-P at its core. It's the reason the Patriots could be a power-run team one week and empty-spread the next with the same playbook.

## Shanahan/McVay: the wide-zone ecosystem

**The idea.** Mike Shanahan (with line guru Alex Gibbs in Denver) married West Coast passing to the **outside/wide zone run game**: the whole line stretches laterally in unison, the back makes one cut, and undersized-but-athletic linemen chop down pursuit. Terrell Davis and a decade of 1,000-yard Broncos backs proved the run game was plug-and-play. His son Kyle, plus Sean McVay, Matt LaFleur, and their sprawling coaching tree, turned it into the modern NFL's dominant ecosystem: **every pass is dressed as the run**.

**The DNA markers.** Under-center snaps at league-leading rates, 21 and 12 personnel with a fullback or sniffer TE, condensed splits, heavy pre-snap motion, and a play-action game (keepers/boots, deep overs, the Yankee post-cross shot) whose backfield action is frame-for-frame identical to outside zone. The QB spends half his dropbacks with his back to the defense — which is fine, because the scheme's whole point is that the defense's eyes are wrong.

**The tree.** Kyle Shanahan (49ers), Sean McVay (Rams, Super Bowl LVI), Matt LaFleur (Packers), Zac Taylor (Bengals), Kevin O'Connell (Vikings, whose 2024 work with Sam Darnold showed the system's QB-elevating power), Mike McDaniel (Miami, the speed-in-space branch with Tyreek Hill), Ben Johnson (whose Lions offenses added gap-scheme physicality and league-famous trick plays before Chicago hired him in 2025). This tree has supplied a huge share of recent head-coach hires; the league copies what wins.

## Spread, option, and RPO influence

The college spread contributed three permanent NFL fixtures:

1. **The zone read**: the QB reads an unblocked edge defender — hand off if he crashes, keep if he stays. It gives the offense an extra blocker's worth of math without blocking anyone.
2. **The RPO (run-pass option)**: a run play with a route attached; the QB reads a second-level defender and either hands off or throws the slant/glance *behind* him. If the linebacker fits the run, throw; if he drops, hand off. He's wrong either way. (The NFL's stricter linemen-downfield rule — ineligible men may only be one yard downfield at the throw, vs. three in college — keeps NFL RPOs quicker and more constrained.)
3. **QB-run structure as identity**: Lamar Jackson's Ravens under OC Todd Monken (and previously Greg Roman) are the fullest expression — pistol alignments, counts the defense can't win because the QB is a true runner, and play-action off it. Jalen Hurts's Eagles added the "tush push" and a gap-scheme QB-run game to a spread passing structure. Josh Allen's Bills and the Steelers' 2025 usage of mobile QBs show milder doses.

Almost no NFL team is a "spread offense" wholesale, but every team's early-down menu now includes zone-read window dressing and a handful of RPOs, and defenses must practice against them weekly.

## Reid's Kansas City hybrid

Andy Reid deserves his own branch because he's the league's great synthesizer. A Holmgren/Walsh West Coast disciple by training, Reid spent his Eagles years as a pass-first West Coast coach, then in Kansas City **fused West Coast structure with Air Raid concepts (hello, Mahomes), college option and jet-motion ideas, and shot plays off deep crossers**. The Chiefs' championship run — Super Bowl titles in the 2019, 2022, and 2023 seasons and a near-miss dynasty capping in the 2024 season's Super Bowl loss to Philadelphia — was built on being unpreparable: spread empty one series, heavy motion trickery the next. When people say "modern NFL offense," they usually mean the Reid blend: concept-named, motion-rich, QB-empowered, shameless about stealing from every tree including high school film.

## Spotting the families in data

You don't need film to classify an offense — the tendencies give it away. A few fingerprints, all visible in the staff profiles on the [Coaches](/coaches) page:

| Signal | What it separates |
|--------|-------------------|
| **PROE** (pass rate over expectation) | Air Raid/Reid-blend teams live positive; run-identity wide-zone and QB-run teams live negative. The metric adjusts for situation, so it's a *philosophy* readout, not a game-script artifact. |
| **Play-action rate** | Shanahan-tree offenses are perennially top of the league; pure dropback spread teams sit low. |
| **Under-center rate** | The cleanest Shanahan-tree tell — nobody else goes under center anymore. |
| **Motion rate** | McDaniel/Shanahan/Reid offenses at the top; static offenses are usually older trees or bad ones. |
| **Personnel mix** | Heavy 21 = Shanahan branch; heavy 12 = TE-rich hybrid; near-permanent 11 = Reid/Air-Raid-influenced spread. |
| **Pace/tempo** | Air Raid heritage and two-minute mastery show up as fast situation-neutral tempo. |

A worked example: an offense with high under-center rate, top-five play-action, heavy motion, negative PROE, and lots of 21 personnel is Shanahan-tree before you've watched a snap — that profile *is* the scheme. An offense with bottom-five play-action, top-five PROE, and 90% 11 personnel is a dropback spread operation. When a team changes coordinators, watch these numbers move in the first month; scheme shows up in tendencies faster than in results. The tendency data on [Coaches](/coaches) — PROE, motion, play-action, pace by staff — is exactly this fingerprinting, and pairing it with EPA splits tells you whether the identity is *working*, which is where betting and fantasy edges start (see [Betting](/betting) for how market prices react slower than tendencies change).

## The convergence

The honest ending: the families are blurring. Every team runs mesh; every team runs wide zone at least as a changeup; every team names some concepts E-P style; every team carries RPOs. What still distinguishes offenses is the **ratio** — which answers a coach reaches for on 2nd-and-7 when nothing is scripted — and the terminology their QB thinks in. That ratio is exactly what tendency stats measure, which is why the numbers and the film agree far more often than either camp admits.
